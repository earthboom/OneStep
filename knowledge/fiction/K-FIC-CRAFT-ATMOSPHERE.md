# Shape atmosphere through situated perception and consequential detail

## Guidance

### Source-supported basis

The Open University setting exercises connect objects, sensory description and a person's circumstances to perceived place [S09]. In a direct interview, Kim Yeonsu describes how naming and ways of seeing can change a story's meaning [S19]. The interview is one writer's perspective, not an empirical account of every reader.

### Applied procedure

1. Establish what the focal person wants, fears, expects and can physically perceive now.
2. Choose a few specific details that affect navigation, choice, memory or relationship. Include the senses useful to this scene rather than a five-sense quota.
3. Arrange attention and narrative distance so the reader encounters the needed change of mood; a familiar object can acquire a new significance through context.
4. Check where atmosphere supports or interrupts action, and whether explanation adds information beyond the image.

### Exercise and expected artifact

Keep a scene's facts fixed and rewrite its opening attention for two different immediate concerns. Compare which details and interpretations change without changing canon.

## Applicability and exceptions

Atmosphere is not a list of universal emotional symbols. A direct statement of emotion, plain detail or unadorned setting may serve the work. Do not silently promote a focal person's interpretation to objective world fact. The older scene-staging reference retains its own original scope.

Use the current manuscript, author choices and available context as the authority for application. This entry supplies options for a concrete decision, not requirements to apply every device.

## Checks

- Why does this person notice this detail now?
- Does the mood shift follow a changed situation or interpretation?
- What useful effect is lost if an ornamental detail is removed?
