# Use Knox's Decalogue to review mystery elements in any genre

## Guidance

Retrieve this entry when planning, drafting, or revising an investigation, unexplained event, hidden identity, suspicious testimony, or deduction-based reveal, including a single chapter in fantasy, wuxia, romance, or another genre. Decide which question the reader is invited to solve and when its answer is due. A chapter may plant a mystery whose answer comes much later.

### Historical reference

Ronald Knox's Decalogue is associated with the introduction to a detective-story anthology published in 1929; the title's “1928” refers to its selected year [S3]. The following are compressed paraphrases of the numbered rules reproduced in [S1] and [S2], not quotations or modern requirements:

| No. | Historical rule, in brief |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Introduce the culprit early; exclude a culprit whose thoughts readers have followed. |
| 2 | Exclude supernatural agencies. |
| 3 | Allow at most one secret room or passage. |
| 4 | Exclude previously undiscovered poisons and devices requiring a late technical explanation. |
| 5 | Exclude Chinese characters. |
| 6 | No helpful accidents or inexplicably correct intuitions. |
| 7 | The investigator cannot commit the crime. |
| 8 | Immediately disclose discovered clues to readers. |
| 9 | Watson conceals no thoughts and is slightly less intelligent than average readers. |
| 10 | Prepare twins and doubles beforehand. |

Rule 5's racial exclusion is rejected. Record it for historical accuracy, never as casting advice. Avoid ethnic stereotypes as an independent present-day editorial choice; that choice does not turn the historical wording into a different rule. [S2] discusses the rules as playful and breakable; [S1] also cautions against treating them as current absolutes.

### Working adaptation for chapters with mystery elements

The procedure below is this entry's editorial synthesis, not another translation of Knox:

1. **Define the reader's task.** Separate a solvable puzzle from suspense about an unknown outcome, dramatic irony, or an intentionally unresolved mystery. Specify what should be inferable before the reveal; do not promise complete solvability accidentally.
2. **Plan backward from the answer.** In the existing outline or scene brief, connect the hidden event to observable traces, plausible competing explanations, and the evidence that discriminates between them. Introduce the relevant people and mechanisms before they become indispensable.
3. **Track information access.** For each decisive clue, note its actual fact, character discovery, reader-visible passage, initial interpretation, and later use. Keep author, investigator, narrator, and reader knowledge distinct. Readers need the usable observation; they need not receive its correct interpretation immediately.
4. **Make investigation causal.** Intuition may suggest a lead; observation, testing, or reasoning must support the conclusion when fair-play deduction is promised. A lucky discovery can initiate trouble without proving the final answer. Motive or suspicious behavior alone need not identify a unique culprit.
5. **Prepare the mechanism within the world.** For magic, qi, technology, disguises, hidden routes, or unusual substances, establish the solution-relevant capabilities and limits early enough to reason about them. Preserve established canon; do not invent a last-minute exception to rescue the explanation. A numerical limit of one hidden passage is not imposed on modern worldbuilding.
6. **Control misdirection and payoff.** Let an observed fact support a tempting mistaken interpretation, or give a witness a grounded reason to lie. At the reveal, reconcile significant contradictory evidence and explain why the chosen answer beats live alternatives. Avoid a decisive clue introduced only in retrospective explanation.

## Applicability and exceptions

Apply this as a reference when mystery reasoning matters, even if the overall work is not detective fiction. Current author intent, viewpoint, voice, canon, and the agreed reader experience govern its use. Do not insert a compulsory murder, investigator/Watson pair, suspect count, or solution into an unrelated chapter.

Rules 1, 7, and 9 do not automatically forbid a culprit viewpoint, a guilty investigator, or an unreliable narrator in contemporary fiction. If those devices are chosen, review what the narrative claims, what it withholds, and whether a reread supports the intended effect. Do not flatten a character's intelligence to satisfy rule 9. Such choices are deliberate departures from the historical rules, not claims that Knox explicitly allowed them.

Fantasy, supernatural mystery, and SF may use extraordinary causes; their discoverable operation and relevant constraints replace the historical blanket ban in this adaptation. Horror or literary ambiguity may preserve uncertainty. If a work deliberately denies a fair-play solution, judge the promised effect and internal consistency rather than silently rewriting it into a puzzle.

Foreshadowing can span chapters. At an intermediate chapter, check the planned clue/reveal schedule; at the actual payoff, check the complete relevant sequence. Do not reveal the answer early merely to make a local checklist pass.

## Checks

Before drafting, record the mystery question, intended payoff chapter, reader contract, applicable world rules, and any deliberate departure in the existing scene brief. Retrieve only the relevant surrounding passages and canon.

After drafting or revision, reread the affected sequence and ask:

- Can every indispensable deduction be traced to a reader-visible passage available before the payoff, under the chosen fair-play contract?
- Does each character acquire and use information at a supported time, without author knowledge leaking into the viewpoint?
- Are a hidden route, special power, twin, disguise, substance, or device prepared before it carries the solution?
- Does the conclusion survive plausible alternatives, rather than merely matching a suspect's motive or a lucky guess?
- Are significant red herrings and apparent contradictions accounted for, with fact distinguished from interpretation or testimony?
- On reread, do the setup, information limits, reveal, and character behavior remain consistent without damaging voice, pacing, or intended ambiguity?

These are proposed editorial checks. Catalog validation verifies storage structure and references, not puzzle solvability, literary quality, or reader response.
