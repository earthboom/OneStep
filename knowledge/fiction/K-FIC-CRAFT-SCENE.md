# Build a scene around its purpose, immediate aim and consequential change

## Guidance

### Source-supported basis

Writing Excuses discusses varying definitions of a scene and asks writers to identify its plot function, protagonist goal and useful starting/stopping points [S03]. The proposed procedure below uses that teaching as a planning and revision aid; it does not require every scene to end in disaster.

### Applied procedure

1. Record the entering state: viewpoint, place, time, relevant knowledge and relationship pressure.
2. Name the local intention and what prevents an effortless result, including uncertainty or an emotional difficulty when relevant.
3. Choose the action, observation, conversation or reaction that changes the next available choice.
4. Begin and stop where the reader gains the necessary experience. Summarize, bridge or cut uneventful transitions while retaining needed orientation.

### Exercise and expected artifact

Make a before/after card for one existing scene, then try two alternative entry points and compare the information and emotional preparation each preserves.

## Applicability and exceptions

A rest, ritual or atmosphere scene may matter through attention or relationship rather than external conflict. Scene/sequel is one model, not a mandatory alternating pattern. Do not cut a necessary reaction solely to accelerate action.

Use the current manuscript, author choices and available context as the authority for application. This entry supplies options for a concrete decision, not requirements to apply every device.

## Checks

- What would disappear from the reader's experience if this scene were removed?
- Is the next decision supported by the scene's changed state?
- Can readers orient themselves after the chosen transition?
