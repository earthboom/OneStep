# Let dialogue pursue an aim while context makes subtext legible

## Guidance

### Source-supported basis

Writing Excuses locates subtext in the interaction of words and context rather than an invisible meaning the reader must guess without clues [S17]. Its tension discussion places the same exchange under differing stakes and audiences [S07]. These ideas support testing what the listener can infer and what the speaker is trying to do.

### Applied procedure

1. State what each participant wants from the exchange and what they can or cannot safely say.
2. Choose a tactic such as reassurance, evasion, bargaining, challenge or confession according to the relationship and immediate pressure.
3. Place necessary clues in wording, action, silence, prior knowledge or discrepancy; keep the inference accessible to the intended reader.
4. Let a response change the tactic or stakes. Review diction and rhythm against the character's established voice and the work's atmosphere.

### Exercise and expected artifact

Write an exchange in which two people need different outcomes from the same request. Annotate their aims separately, then remove explanation only where the actual clues carry the inference.

## Applicability and exceptions

Direct, sincere speech is valid; subtext is not compulsory deception. Repeated mannerisms, dialect caricature or universal aphorisms do not by themselves distinguish voices. Apply the existing selected-dialogue adaptation procedure when references are used, without copying their distinctive wording.

Use the current manuscript, author choices and available context as the authority for application. This entry supplies options for a concrete decision, not requirements to apply every device.

## Checks

- What is each line trying to obtain, conceal or change?
- What textual or contextual cue supports the intended inference?
- Could another character say this unchanged, and would that fit the scene?
