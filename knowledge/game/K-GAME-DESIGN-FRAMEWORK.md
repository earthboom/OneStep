# Choose genre methods through intended experience and observable play

## Guidance

Source observation: Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek distinguish rules, behavior arising during play, and desired player responses. Their framework connects these perspectives and explicitly offers no formula guaranteeing fun [S1].

Proposed use: describe the intended experience in concrete terms, then map a playable sequence: available information -> action -> system response -> changed options. Identify what players should be able to learn, express, coordinate, or overcome. Name an observable behavior that would support or undermine that intention. Choose genre references matching the bottleneck: readable response windows for combat, competing allocations for strategy, inference for puzzles, or information pacing for horror.

For a hybrid, identify the primary activity and supporting systems. Check where a supporting reward, timer, resource limit, or disclosure changes the intended experience. Keep the existing project design authoritative and trace decisions through its system documents rather than creating a second design bible.

## Applicability and exceptions

MDA is a conceptual lens, not an empirical law or a required checklist of emotions. Its aesthetics concern player responses, not only graphics. Neither every game nor every moment requires a new reward or difficult choice. Apply source examples within their design context; platform, player group, competitive structure, and accessibility can change the appropriate method.

## Checks

Proposed checks: can a representative sequence demonstrate the intended experience? Are the important choices, information, failure/recovery paths, and feedback visible? Does a local optimization undermine the whole loop? A genre label or coherent diagram does not establish actual enjoyment; record the build, participants, conditions and observation when playtesting becomes authorized.
