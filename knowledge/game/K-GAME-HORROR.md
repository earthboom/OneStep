# Uncertainty and horror pacing

## Guidance

**Source-supported:** Thomas Grip's Frictional Games retrospective reports that frequent scares, exploitable threats, and absorbing combat or puzzles could undermine the studio's intended experience. It argues for narrative context, a defined player role, imperfect information, and agency [1]. Motive describes a different implementation for Dead Space: its Intensity Director combines environmental events and enemy spawning, monitors intensity, and alternates peaks with breaks. The team reports extensive iteration [2]. These accounts support multiple approaches rather than a prohibition on combat.

**Reusable planning synthesis:** State what the player should fear, why their character must engage, and which actions remain possible. Separate the unknown threat from the usable information needed to navigate and make decisions. For each encounter specify the warning, interpretation, choice, possible consequence, escape or coping route, and recovery period. Suspense needs something the player can anticipate; unreadable controls merely obscure action.

Map a chapter's quiet investigation, ambiguous signs, commitment points, active danger, and decompression. Give quiet content a purpose: understanding the setting, reconsidering a prior event, preparing, or choosing the next risk. A scare budget should account for attention and habituation, not only event frequency. Persistent resource pressure can make relief incomplete without requiring constant pursuit.

Prototype a short sequence before building a full scare library. For dynamic events, define eligibility, repetition limits, competing cues, and recovery rules so combinations remain navigable. For authored events, test approach directions and player delays. Preserve the difference between intended uncertainty and a threat that becomes harmless when its limitations are discovered.

## Applicability and exceptions

Frictional's examples favor vulnerability and narrative attention; action-horror may deliberately reward mastery as well. Multiplayer conversation, repeated attempts, and speedrunning can change the emotional goal. Darkness and ambiguity can undermine accessibility or fairness. No fixed amount of fear, number of scares, or universal tension curve is established by these sources.

## Checks

Proposed checks: observe first and repeat attempts; record anticipated danger, actual response, avoidance, and moments of confusion. Try obvious safe-position exploits. Inspect whether resource failure permits meaningful decisions. Review randomized combinations and quiet intervals as complete sequences. Treat fear reports and completion behavior as separate observations; no such sessions ran in this research.
