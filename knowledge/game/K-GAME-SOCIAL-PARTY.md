# Participation and shared reveals

## Guidance

**Source-supported:** Jackbox's Blather 'Round account describes testing the core mechanic with paper, moving to a digital prototype, and subsequently changing or removing features [1]. The Roomerang diary reports that external feedback changed score communication; internal tests exposed a desire to read elimination reasons and prompted reconsideration of reveal pacing. Its developers also describe repeated work on challenge, advantage, and voting clarity [2]. These are production accounts, not controlled evidence that one social formula succeeds everywhere.

**Reusable planning synthesis:** Specify the social exchange: who creates something, who sees it, who interprets or votes, who benefits, and what becomes public afterward. Record the information held privately and shared by the group at every phase. Plan the opportunities for a quieter player to act, understand the result, and influence the next round; laughter alone does not reveal whose experience is working.

Run the smallest paper version using prompts, slips, and a moderator. Preserve simultaneous or secret decisions when they matter to the mechanic. Use the test to decide whether the exchange creates the intended interaction before adding presentation. Move to devices when private screens, entry friction, input limits, latency, or public display change that interaction.

Structure rounds as understandable instruction, contribution, anticipation, reveal, consequence, and return to participation. Allocate time for reading player-generated content and reactions while limiting passive waiting. Decide whether elimination removes agency, shifts a player's role, or ends the session. Curate prompts for varied familiarity and expression; budget localization and repeated-play variety alongside code and art.

## Applicability and exceptions

Useful for short group games built around communication and interpretation. These sources do not establish hidden-role balance, tournament fairness, moderation policy, or matchmaking design. Friends, strangers, remote callers, and mixed-language groups may behave differently. More rules can enrich strategy but raise onboarding and explanation costs. A successful paper exchange does not verify device usability or online timing.

## Checks

Proposed checks: observe unfamiliar groups without rescuing every rule explanation; record misunderstood phases, contribution distribution, waiting, unread reveals, and disengagement after elimination. Compare scores as understood by players with actual scoring. Test minimum and maximum intended group sizes and one repeat session. These are proposed checks; no group results were fabricated.
