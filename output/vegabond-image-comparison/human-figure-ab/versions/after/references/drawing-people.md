# Drawing people

Start with the action a viewer should recognize. Choose a single instant, a viewpoint and the parts that need to remain visible. Use only the cues that explain that instant. Keep a person's age, build, complexion, distinctive features and intended stylization throughout revisions. A supplied reference can establish pose, identity or lighting; specify which.

## What holds the body up?

Read the silhouette at delivery size. For weight-bearing poses, identify the support and follow it through ankle, knee and pelvis, or through the seat into the torso. In motion, show which foot is accepting weight and which is releasing it. A raised heel can be right for a departing foot. A carried load should pull on the grip and affect the arm or balance in a way that fits its weight. Let fabric compress at a bent joint or pull toward a support where it helps reveal the pose.

Trace each visible limb back to its owner across overlaps. Account for foreshortening and body variation. Inspect the joint where a limb disappears and where it reappears. If an interaction matters, frame it large enough to read and preserve it when cropping.

## What makes contact?

For a grasp or exchange, name the job of each hand and what carries the weight at this moment. Trace forearm, wrist, palm, and the fingers that are visible. Look at which side of the prop they meet, what passes in front, what passes behind, and whether the contact closes. A palm supporting the underside of a cup, for example, should meet the base while the other person's fingers wrap around the handle. Naturally hidden digits can stay hidden. For fine work, check where a lace, tool or handle enters and leaves the grasp.

For a portrait, trace the turn from shoulders through neck and jaw. Give the person something specific to attend to. Check whether both eyes aim toward that target from the chosen viewpoint and whether eyelids, cheeks and mouth support the requested expression. Preserve individual asymmetry.

## What is coloring the skin?

Preserve the person's underlying complexion. Identify the main source and any visibly relevant fill or bounce. Track one light across a cheek, the turn under the jaw and the neck, then compare a nearby collar, hand or prop in the same light. Allow real differences between face, lips, palms and forearms. An unexplained color jump or identical painted-on blush across separate planes warrants a closer look. Under mixed light, locate each source in the space and let it fall off as the surface turns. Keep detail in the shadows of deep skin tones and retain the shape of a bright reflection.

At actual viewing size, skin texture should follow scale, focus, medium and age. Check a lit plane beside a shadow plane for waxy smoothing, uniform stamped pores or equally sharp wrinkles everywhere. In watercolor a transparent wash turning around the jaw can do the work of fine photographic texture.

## Inspect and repair

First read the action in the whole frame. Then enlarge the contact or facial feature that could invalidate it and compare adjacent skin regions in the same light. Describe the visible problem and its location. For an isolated fault, edit the smallest useful region that includes both sides of an interaction, such as the hand and the cup rim; preserve expression, identity, light and framing. If the underlying pose cannot carry the action, restage from the last good source. Recheck contacts, skin and the original brief on the selected image.
